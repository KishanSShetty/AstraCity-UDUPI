AstraCity: A Ward-Level Digital Twin for Municipal Solid Waste Management Integrating Remote Sensing, Route Optimization, and Environmental Impact Analysis
Kishan Shetty — RV College of Engineering, kishanshetty.udupika20@gmail.com

Abstract
AstraCity is a digital twin system for the management of municipal solid waste (MSW), designed for the ward level, which combines geospatial observation, waste flow estimation, route optimization and environment impacts analysis into an integrated decision support system. The twin mirrors are applied on HSR Layout, Bengaluru, and the geo-referenced buildings, road classified segments, and six Dry Waste Collection Centres (DWCCs) serve as the dataset. Maximum likelihood classification (MLC) applied to sentinel-2 imagery identifies 15.3% of open land and the classification is checked against a risk of illegal dump; this is done by applying a DBSCAN clustering algorithm. 54 scenarios are used for modelling across waste generation. A Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (CVRPTW) with solution based on the Clarke–Wright Savings heuristic is able to minimise fleet distance per day by 75.5% (32.41 km out of 132.44 km). The First-Order Decay based estimates of the IPCC are Tier-1, ranging from 25,959 tonnes CO₂e per year monetised at ₹5.19 crores/year (927 tonnes CH₄/year). Ward savings over a year amount to an estimated of ₹5.79 crores. The framework illustrates the combined use of satellite remote sensing, application of combinatorial optimization and environmental accounting as a single decision support architecture for civic managing units.

1. Introduction
Despite the estimated huge amount of MSW generated by urban municipalities across India (more than 150,000 tonnes per day), only a small fraction of cities utilise modern tools for Witjak (short for Collection Plan) such as paper maps, complaint registers, and institutional memory (CPCB, 2022). The BBMP budget on solid waste operations is in the hundreds of crores per year, with challenges yet to be ironed out, such as routes that were created 20 years ago, no real-time detection system to identify the locations of new illegal dump sites and no system to estimate the level of methane at a single ward or in a city.
Not having spatial intelligence in administrative decision making is the root cause. As long as officials can't picture a gradient of waste, they can tap a toe against the wall when it comes to assigning vehicles, they can do nothing response model for financial implications and when policy decisions, waste management stays reactive.
This is where AstraCity comes in: AstraCity builds a virtual, continuously queryable Ward 174 that mirrors the physical assets, models and optimizes operations, and estimates environmental impact. The twin is not a map dashboard, rather, it is a layered decision support framework in which each analytical module supports the next.
The four capabilities that are integrated into one pipeline are the core contribution:
The illegal dump risk screening (spatial surveillance layer) is derived from the Sentinel-2 LULC classification.
2. Waste modelling at the building level (flow layer) and the simulation of scenarios.
CVRPTW routing, both under road-width and time constraints, including optimization layer (3).
4.	Estimate methane from IPCC models and economic scenarios (impact layer)
HSR Layout (Ward 174) was chosen for the pilot area due to a combination of a high availability of data, coupled with structural features which are representative of the problems faced in dealing with a municipal solid waste catchment in Bengaluru and its narrowness to allow for detailed digital twinning. This was based on three criteria. The ward's urban morphology, defined as high residential density, mixed commercial corridors, a road network divided between wide arterial and narrow internal roads as well as open land (15.3%), is the same as the one observed in many wards of BBMP, hence findings are transferable. Second, Ward 174 has documented operational relevance; the persistent existence of garbage hotspot areas, discrepancies in sub-zone segregation and a DWCC capacity shortage, compared to the demand of dry waste, is documented in BBMP micro-plans and ward performance reports, and therefore represents a realistic intervention context rather than a hypothetical one. Third, the ward has open and reproducible data, such as BBMP solid waste micro-plans, OpenStreetMap building and road layers, and Sentinel2 imagery which is clipped to the ward boundary without proprietary rights. The proposed pipeline is reliant on ward-level input (which is the data available to them) only, thus it is possible to replicate the pipeline across other wards in BBMP with similar availability.

3. Related Work
3.1 Emission-Capacitated Vehicle Routing
For CVRP, typical formulations are those of minimizing distance or travel time. A further view employed is the concept of the "emission capacitated variant", where per-vehicle CO₂ emissions account for the total effect through considering the fleet composition as well as the vehicle route length. Rabbani et al. [14] develop a green VRP for waste collection problem which minimizes a combination of distance and vehicle emissions, demonstrating that emission savings are more pronounced for small decreases in route length when replacing heavy compactors with lightweight vehicles in short urban distances. This incentivizes the emission-weighted approach of AstraCity's emission objective, which applies emission-factors per vehicle type (kg CO₂/km) directly per route edge instead of calculating them after-the-fact.
A. Estimate nonlinear tour times using a regression approach on Avoidance Data.Avoidance Data: Estimate nonlinear tour times via a regression approach.
Trifa et al. [15] show that introducing time-of-day speed variation to CVRPTW changes the feasibility and overall emissions, especially with high congestion periods occurring during morning rush hours – the same time window (06:00–10:00 hours) employed for the collection time in this study. They assign speed profiles to the road classes/periods where they are applied, which give more realistic arrival-time accumulation for longer routes. AstraCity has a two-speed road network (06:00 to 08:00 low-, 08:00 to 10:00 peak) for each road category.
3.3 IoT-Enabled Smart Bin Systems
Fill-level sensor networks on bins and DWCCs minimise unnecessary collection trips by sending dynamic updates of the routes whenever bins are over a fill threshold [16]. In an architecture presented by Catania and Ventura [17] sensor readings in the bins are read at regular intervals at a routing engine, which provides continual updates on route assignments without reprovisioning. From the current configuration of AstraCity, this integration is considered as a design extension in that it incorporates the data schema and decision triggers that will happen in the event of IoT deployment, but for the purpose of this study the existing scenario based waste estimates are used to represent actual sensor readings.

4. Methodology
3.1 Study Area and Data Sources
HSR Layout is a new township in south-east of Bengaluru situated between ORR corridors and is 7.0 km² in size with 174 ward, with the centroid (North latitude and East longitude) at 12.915N, 77.640E. It consists 9,471 building footprints (94.8% independent houses, apartments, commercial, IT and institutional uses), an estimated population of ~1,10,000 and a daily waste load of 55 tonnes. There is no on-ward dumpyard/BMU/processing plant and wet waste is transported 2.10 km from the ward to Kudlu Gate BMU and the inert fraction is transported 21.03 km from the ward to Yelahanka dumpyard. During surge periods there is a structural deficit of 6 DWCCs for the total estimated capacity of ~15 t/day dry-waste versus a total demand of ~16.5 t/day.
All data were open access and 18 datasets were used: Sentinel-2 Level-2A images (ESA Copernicus), building footprints (OSM via Overpass API), road data (GeoFabrik export) and DWCC, BMU and dumpyard locations (BBMP GIS shapefiles). The ward was used as a basis for population estimates based on Census 2011 ward data and scaled using building occupancy models (4 persons/independent house, 284 persons/apartment).
The classification of LULC relies on remote sensing data and photos.LULC classification is based on the remote sensing data and photos.
The Sentinel-2 Level-2A imagery (ESA Copernicus) were then imported into QGIS 3.x and classified using the Semi-Automatic Classification Plugin (SCP) using Maximum Likelihood Classification (MLC). The following four classes were identified: Built-up, Vegetation, Open Land, Water Bodies. Eight to 15 polygons were digitised for each of training regions of interest to provide sufficient spectral diversity, across the four quadrants of the ward. Stratified random sampling (with samples >50 points per class) was used to validate classification accuracy for overall accuracy >85% and Kappa >0.80. An automatic 3×3 majority filter reduced the salt-and-pepper noise prior to saving/export.
The key output that feeds into the next module is Open Land (15.3%, 2.83 km²) as these areas are the main areas for illegal dump formation.
Until now, the patrol service has been drilled by what amounts to a dogma.The patrol service was drilled by what amounts to “dogma” until now.
To avoid dumping anomalies in open land polygons generated by LULC classification, a brightness threshold approach was used: the brightness of pixels with values below the 10th percentile of open land distribution within the local neighborhood were marked as dump anomalies. The spatially adjacent candidate points were clustered into discrete dump sites using the DBSCAN. noise pixels were rejected as unrelated noise.
The following four potential sites were detected: two high-risk (P0 - same-day cleanup) and two medium-risk (P1, 48 hours response). This output is used to help determine how much priority to give a cleanup and input to waste routing module (higher priority in the routing scenarios) to high-risk zones.
3.4 Waste Generation Modelling (Flow Layer)
The ward was broken down into 59 grid cells, of which 59 are active. Per-cell waste generation was determined as:
Waste = (0.45 × P) + (2.8 × C) × multipliers
The equation used to model the population per cell (P = 4 persons/independent house, 284 persons/apartment) is influenced by the multipliers of "growth" (0.0 to 0.5), "surge" (Diwali = ×1.20, Ganesh Chaturthi = ×1.28), and "weather" (low rainfall = ×0.95, heavy rainfall = ×1.15).
A dump-probability index was calculated per cell depending on waste-to-DWCC-capacity ratio, segregation failure rate, open-land fraction and inverse of the road density. This index identifies areas of waste surplus/deficit in conjunction with inadequate infrastructure – the most vulnerable cells in the twin.
A set of 54 pre-computation scenarios (3 growth levels × 3 festival states × 3 weather states) can be explored in real-time without re-computation.

3.5 Route Optimization (CVRPTW Layer)
Formulating the problem: The collection problem is formulated as a Capacitated Vehicle Routing Problem with Time Windows (CVRPTW). Constraints:
All collection zones were visited only one time each day.
No overloading vehicles (per vehicle category)
•	All service within window 06:00–10:00
•	Routes start and end at depot (DWCC: 77.6392°E, 12.9158°N)
Road width < or = to Minimum width of Vehicle (IRC standards) - Restricted by Edge crossers
Two tier fleet structure: Ward 174 has a road network made from 41.4% medium lanes (2-4 m) and 27.6% of footways (<2 m) making for a tiered approach. Tier-1 employs push and throw carts, and auto-tippers on narrow streets and deposit at nodes of transfer (DWCCs or accessible micro-depots). Tier 2: has compactors on wider roads, which go to transfer nodes, which go to BMU/dumpyard. This reflects a pragmatic constraint, that large vehicles cannot be brought into the majority of the ward.
The client-side implementation of the Clarke–Wright Savings heuristic algorithm, in TypeScript. The dimension is defined as savings s(i, j) = d(depot→i) + d(depot→j) − d(i→j) (the cost of two non consecutive hops from depot to i and j minus the cost of a single hop from i to j), the routes are sorted in descending order of savings, and then added iteratively with capacity and time window constraints. Road distances using OSRM API, otherwise using Haversine formula.
Current implementation minimises total distance (proposed extension): emission-weighted objective. What a natural extension (but not implemented) would be to replace the scalar objective with a combination of distance and vehicle emissions weighted against some penalty:
Minimize:  α · Σ(d_ij · x_kij)  +  β · Σ(e_k · d_ij · x_kij)
The emission factors e_k (kg CO₂/km) for each vehicle type k are taken from the IPCC or the manufacturers; the weights α and β are set by the decision maker. AstraCity already has cases with an attribute for vehicleType per route edge - no architectural change is necessary, only that e_k values should be provided, based on field measurement or published fleet data per edge of the route. This extension would have the emissions generated by the fleet as a routing input instead of a post-hoc estimate, and would directly connect the routing module and carbon-credit in Section 4.7. Here, the distance-emissions trade-off is identified as future work, by quantifying it.
Current Clarke–Wright implementation: Implements with static OSRM distances (proposed extension): Time dependent travel times (TDTT). It has been confirmed that during the months between 08:00 and 10:00 (morning peak), vehicle speed is often reduced on corridors adjacent to the ORR on HSR Layouts. A time dependent extension based on Trifa et al. [15] would split the period 06:00-10:00 into two parts and pre-schedule road classes specific speed for each part, thus allowing the times to be accumulated in a more realistic manner to prevent long tours from departing late and making them impractical to schedule before. The architectural portion of this extension is relatively simple based on the existing road classification data; however, the speed calibration of the various categories is done locally with GPS traces or traffic counts which were not available in the present study.

4.6 IoT Integration Layer (Design Specification)
The current twin is scenario dependent: Waste volume, loads on routes and DWCC utilization are computed and not based on actual sensors. A sensor integration layer is mandated as a future deployment objective and three data streams are defined as:
At DWCCs: Fill level or weight sensors (compactor bays) instead of daily tonnage estimation would diminish dependence on CPCB per capita averages. A partial route update for the cluster could be triggered by a fill-threshold (80% daily capacity).
At vehicles: GPS logging per stop would give observed travel times per road segment allowing the time dependent speed model from Section 3.5 to be calibrated.
This would give a ground-truthing of the ward-level waste generation model over time at the BMU/dumpyard: weighbridge readings per arrival of truck.
This study did not involve the collection of sensor data or data simulation. The 54 pre-computed scenarios are currently used as the proxy measure of demand variability. Accepting live feeds as input to the twin is achieved without having to change the data schema for the twin or the analytical pipeline (not a current capability, but intended to be undergone as an upgrade path).
The engineering of the methane estimation and economic assessment (impact layer) will be described.
Methane: IPCC Tier-1 Forward/Optimal (FOD) method:
CH₄ (tonnes/year) = MSW_organic × DOCf × MCF × (16/12) × (1 − OX)
MSW_organic = 12,246 tonnes/year (61% organic fraction × 55 t/day × 365). Parameters for DOCf: 0.5; for MCF: 0.8 (managed anaerobic, Kudlu BMU) and for OX: 0.1 (IPCC default). CO₂e conversion uses GWP100 = 28 (IPCC AR5).

4.5 Module Interaction - How the Twin Closes the Loop
Open land parcels are identified in the LULC classification; converted these to prioritized dump sites in the DBSCAN classification, put the dump-risk scores into the waste generation model, then gives the high risk zones a higher collection frequency to use in the routing module, the results from the routing module (distance, fleet load) fed to the economic model, and the carbon savings were calculated against actual route emissions. When you write an output to one of the modules, this changes the input to the following module so that the twin does not just repeat information but updates each input to the following module.

5. Discussion
5.1 Why a Digital Twin, instead of separate analyses?
This publication breaks the monotony of the analytical five-part format by having the five components placed in a "chaining-like" format rather than in standalone style. The LULC output is not only a land cover map, but also the spatial input file to DBSCAN risk scoring. This is not merely the shortest route but rather how all zones are supplied with enough collection and how all zones have an excess of waste and an increased dump probability.This is not only the shortest path to optimize distance but also to ensure that enough waste goes to each zone and too much waste goes to each ‘extra’ zone with a higher dump probability. The methane is not an "after the fact" calculation, but is correlated to the waste tonnage that the same scenario model underlying routing calculates. This integration is what makes the use of the 'digital twin' concept justified.
5.2 Road Width as a Design ConstraintExplain the significance of a variety of constraints when designing a road.
The lack of segments in Ward 174 being available for conventional trucks (17.4% of segments) will make it impractical to implement any routing solution without being aware of the width constraints. The twin algorithm is putting into the CVRPTW edge set, hard-coded specifications of the width of the road for IRC. The two tiered fleet is not a design choice, but an absolute must because of this constraint.
Selected Topics in Segregation Disparities and Targeted Intervention.
The sub-zone segregation ranges from 38% in the commercial areas to 68% at Northern HSR to 42% at Eastern HSR. The twin's dump-probability index helps to capture this variation because of the distinction of segregation failure rate as a part of the zonal risk, resulting in the risk score of areas with lower segregation rates being higher than the risk of areas with similar waste volumes. This will enable the differentiated intervention of awareness-raising in high compliance areas and audits for obliged commercial bulk generators.
Give an account of the processes of scenario method and the preparation for operations.Explain scenario method/operational preparation.
The 55% increase in case of the heaviest of the rains (worst case scenario) puts the DWCC capacity under stress and infringes on the basic fleet capacity. The twin pre-computes this scenario, detects the 5 bins that need to fill beyond capacity and enables the scheduling of more runs of auto-tippers and advance of BMU scheduling prior to the event. Scenario-simulation layer is based on the paradigm of shifting the reactive crisis management to proactive capacity planning.
The design explanation for the 5.5 Emission-Aware routing is given.
The present goal for the routing is: Minimize the total route length. The fleet is not a standard fleet, because it is heavy compactor (typical range ~0.90 kg CO₂/km), auto-tipper (typical range ~0.35 kg CO₂/km) and push cart (zero direct emission). An optimal solution can have heavy compactors on long routes and the total distance may be lower than the from-emissions perspective, but the per km emissions may be higher. The emission-weighted formulation (in Section 3.5) is explicit and quantitative in expressing this trade-off. The primary difference between distance optimal and emission aware route sets that this framework allows is a comparison; the difference in total CO₂ and total carbon which credit value can be calculated. The present study did not conduct any comparisons.
When is Traveling Salesman Problem desirable? Open Questions
The most eastern part of Ward 174 are the furthest from the depot and lie within one of the corridors used by ORR – with notable morning peak congestion. Routes are determined in an optimal manner to these zones according to the present static distance model, thus it is likely that users will experience longer than optimal times for their journeys in the 08:00-10:00 peak period. Whether the time-window violations that are observed in practice are also observed in codes in use and what the feasibility of resequencing zones (entering the western zones first, leaving them last at 06:00 before an area reaches its peak congestion) is remains an empirical question.Empirically, there is no answer whether the time-dependent extension (as described in section 3.5) reduces the time-window violation, as shown in practice, or whether it makes the time-dependent extension more feasible (e.g. resequencing the furthest zone first and takeoff immediately after the congestion peak). This comparison would take place with local information on speed (GPS traces or traffic surveys) which were not collected in this study.
5.7 IoT Integration Pathway
Because of the uncertainty on daily use, the spread of the scenario for the twin is not measured, but instead the baseline - worst case waste range is 55%. Most future impactful first step in starting the deployment of IoT is to install the fill level sensors at six DWCCs: Replace the average filling rate as reported by CPCB with the reported actual filling rate through fill level sensors at DWCCs on a daily basis. The planning uncertainty would be reduced by not making changes to either the route or methane modules (they both handle the number of waste per cell) Further GPS integration would validate the speed model (with its variation over time). The data schema for the twin is the same as that used in these feeds in order to not require the re-architect of the system due to incremental nature of sensor-architecture.
5.8 Limitations
No efforts were undertaken to validate any 'ground truth' clean-up sites identified by a satellite sensor in this study. The average/standard value for any Waste Generation parameter is not the average/ward specific value obtained from the weighbridges in the ward but Average of CPCB. Observation of the field, rather than systematic survey defines the method of estimation of segregation rate. The price of carbon credit is an emerging-market price range – use as a planning price range estimate. The single major future improvement recommended is for integration with shop-floor sensor systems (IoT) like bin fill level or truck-GPS that would turn the twin into a near real-time system.

6. Conclusion
AstraCity presents a proof-of-concept of a municipal solid waste management (MSWM) digital twin that can be created with no proprietary software or real-time sensor networks. The twin results in a 75.5% reduction in daily fleet distance in Ward 174 (HSR Layout, Bengaluru); identifies four hazardous areas for dumping (dumping that is prohibited in the area); identifies annual CO₂e emissions at 25,959tCO₂e; and estimates the annual ward-level saving at ₹5.79 crore.
The contribution to the main product is basically architectural, meaning that remote sensing, spatial risk screening, and modelling of wastes based on scenarios will occur in one single decision support pipeline whose output will feed into another's input. In addition to the core that has been implemented, there are three extensions formally identified but not yet calculated: an emission-weighted routing objective which would enable fleet CO₂ to be used as a route time constraint, not as an estimate; a time-dependent travel-time model to assess if time-window violations occur under morning-peak traffic; a sensor integration layer whose data schema is formally defined and able to receive live feeds for DWCC and vehicles. These extensions are extensions of the present, rather than flights of fancy.
